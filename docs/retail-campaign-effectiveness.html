1. Background

Retailers often invest in direct-marketing campaigns, such as coupons. Households are given coupons as part of these marketing scheme but the question is:

Do these campaigns increase household spend, and do all household types respond in the same way?

This first four sections of this project explain the data, check its quality, describe how households shop and the campaigns looked at in this analysis. Later sections will measure the effect of the campaigns themselves. Each section follows the same pattern: what we look at, what we found, and what it means for the campaign analysis.

About the data

The data covers two years of grocery shopping by 2500 households who shop regularly at one U.S. retailer. It records every product they bought, as well as which households were targeted by 30 direct-marketing campaigns and which coupons were redeemed.

Key terms

  • Household: one shopping household, identified by a household key.
  • Basket: one shopping trip. All line items bought together share the same basket ID.
  • Line item: one product on one receipt. Each row of the transactions table is a line item.
  • Campaign: a direct-marketing effort sent to chosen households, usually with coupons.

Variables in the transactions data set

The two key variable sin the transactions data set are sales_value (the money received for a line) and quantity (the number of units bought). Two units of a $1.50 item is a quantity of 2 and a sales value of about $3.00. Spend in this report is measured with sales value. There are no negative sales values, so no returns or refunds are recorded as negative sales. However, 18,850 lines (0.73% of all lines) have a sales value of zero, and 14,466 lines (0.56%) have a quantity of zero.

What this means for the analysis:

  • Spend: Lines with zero sales add nothing to a household’s total spend, so they don’t distort spend totals.
  • Averages per line: They do pull the average line value down slightly, because they count as lines. Household and basket totals are not affected.
  • Units: A quantity of zero can’t be used to work out a price per unit, because dividing by zero gives an error. Quantity is not used for that in this report.
  • Scale: Both groups are under 1% of the data, so the main results are very unlikely to change. These rows are kept in, and flagged here for transparency.

3. Descriptive Statistics

This section describes how much households buy and how much their spending varies. In the tables, Mean (SD) is the average with the spread around it, and Median is the middle value. When a few large values pull the mean up, the median is a better picture of a typical case.

4. Campaigns

Before measuring any effect, we need to know what the campaigns were: how many, what type, when they ran and how many households each one targeted.

Campaign overview

Lists each campaign with its type, timing and reach, in the order they started. Weeks are worked out from the day numbers so they match the weekly charts.

Show code 
# Count how many different households each campaign targeted
campaign_targets <- campaign_hh %>%
  group_by(campaign) %>%
  summarise(households_targeted = n_distinct(household_key))

# Join the counts to the campaign descriptions and work out weeks and length
campaign_overview <- campaign_desc %>%
  left_join(campaign_targets, by = "campaign") %>%
  mutate(
    start_week  = ceiling(start_day / 7),     # day 1 to 7 is week 1
    end_week    = ceiling(end_day / 7),
    length_days = end_day - start_day + 1
  ) %>%
  arrange(start_day) %>%
  select(campaign, description, start_week, end_week, length_days, households_targeted)

# Display in kable

# 1. Background

Retailers often invest in **direct-marketing campaigns**, such as
coupons. Households are given coupons as part of these marketing scheme
but the question is:

> Do these campaigns increase household spend, and do all household
> types respond in the same way?

This first four sections of this project explain the data, check its
quality, describe how households shop and the campaigns looked at in
this analysis. Later sections will measure the effect of the campaigns
themselves. Each section follows the same pattern: what we look at, what
we found, and what it means for the campaign analysis.

## About the data

The data covers two years of grocery shopping by 2500 households who
shop regularly at one U.S. retailer. It records every product they
bought, as well as which households were targeted by 30 direct-marketing
campaigns and which coupons were redeemed.

**`r big(sales_quantity_values$zero_sales)`** lines
(`r scales::percent(sales_quantity_values$zero_sales / nrow(transactions), accuracy = 0.01)`
of all lines) have a sales value of zero, and
**`r big(sales_quantity_values$zero_quantity)`** lines
(`r scales::percent(sales_quantity_values$zero_quantity / nrow(transactions), accuracy = 0.01)`)
have a quantity of zero.

**What this means for the analysis:**

- **Spend:** Lines with zero sales add nothing to a household's total
  spend, so they don't distort spend totals.
- **Averages per line:** They do pull the average line value down
  slightly, because they count as lines. Household and basket totals are
  not affected.
- **Units:** A quantity of zero can't be used to work out a price per
  unit, because dividing by zero gives an error. Quantity is not used
  for that in this report.
- **Scale:** Both groups are under 1% of the data, so the main results
  are very unlikely to change. These rows are kept in, and flagged here
  for transparency.

# 4. Campaigns

Before measuring any effect, we need to know what the campaigns were:
how many, what type, when they ran and how many households each one
targeted.

## Campaign overview

Lists each campaign with its type, timing and reach, in the order they
started. Weeks are worked out from the day numbers so they match the
weekly charts.

```{r campaign-overview}
# Count how many different households each campaign targeted
campaign_targets <- campaign_hh %>%
  group_by(campaign) %>%
  summarise(households_targeted = n_distinct(household_key))

# Join the counts to the campaign descriptions and work out weeks and length
campaign_overview <- campaign_desc %>%
  left_join(campaign_targets, by = "campaign") %>%
  mutate(
    start_week  = ceiling(start_day / 7),     # day 1 to 7 is week 1
    end_week    = ceiling(end_day / 7),
    length_days = end_day - start_day + 1
  ) %>%
  arrange(start_day) %>%
  select(campaign, description, start_week, end_week, length_days, households_targeted)

# Display in kable
campaign_overview %>%
  kable(caption = "The campaigns in order of start date",
        col.names = c("Campaign", "Type", "Start week", "End week", "Length (days)", "Households targeted"),
        align = c("l", "l", "r", "r", "r", "r")) %>%
  style_table()
```

## Campaign types

The campaigns come in three types, labelled A, B and C. They differ in
how coupons were given out:

- **Type A:** each household in the campaign received 16 coupons, chosen
  from a pool of coupons according to what that household had bought
  before.
- **Type B and Type C:** every household in the campaign received all of
  the coupons for that campaign.

This matters for the analysis. Type A coupons were matched to each
household's past buying, so households may already have been likely to
buy those products. Results are therefore compared by campaign type as
well as overall.

```{r campaign-types}
# Summarise the campaigns by type: how many, how long, how many households targeted
campaign_types <- campaign_overview %>%
  group_by(description) %>%
  summarise(

    `Households targeted, mean (SD)` = paste0(big(round(mean(households_targeted))), " (",
                                              big(round(sd(households_targeted))), ")")
  )
```
